# Episode 44: Theories of Wisdom - Summary

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cK5Npv-OdAE) · [Read transcript](https://www.meaningcrisis.co/ep-44-awakening-from-the-meaning-crisis-theories-of-wisdom/)

## 1.1 Episode Synopsis

Episode 44 surveys contemporary psychological theories of wisdom, examining four major frameworks: Schwartz & Sharpe's virtue-based account, Baltes & Staudinger's Berlin paradigm, Monika Ardelt's three-dimensional model, and Robert Sternberg's balance theory. Vervaeke extracts insights while identifying critical gaps across all approaches. His central critique is that these are "product theories" describing what wisdom is, while lacking "process theories" that explain how one becomes wise—the developmental and transformational dimension central to ancient wisdom traditions. He argues that all four theories implicitly or explicitly invoke relevance realization machinery: the capacity to zero in on what is significant, balance competing demands, and grasp meaning beyond mere propositional knowledge. Vervaeke advocates for integrating these product theories with a process theory that includes an independent account of foolishness (not merely "lack of wisdom"), the mechanisms of self-deception, and the transformative practices that move people from illusion to reality. The episode positions wisdom as requiring not just theoretical knowledge about wisdom, but participatory transformation—becoming a different kind of person through self-transcendence. This sets the stage for Vervaeke's own integrative account in subsequent episodes, connecting wisdom to relevance realization, meaning in life, and the larger project of awakening from the meaning crisis.

## 1.2 Ontological Moves (Primary Thread)

- **Product vs. Process Theory as an ontological distinction**: Vervaeke's central move is distinguishing theories that define wisdom as a static state or set of characteristics (product theories) from those that explain wisdom as an ongoing developmental process of transformation. This reflects a deeper ontological commitment: is wisdom primarily a possession (knowledge about wisdom, criteria satisfied) or a mode of being (ongoing participatory transformation)? The episode argues that contemporary psychology has unconsciously adopted a "wisdom-as-possession" ontology while the ancient traditions operated from a "wisdom-as-becoming" ontology.

- **The modal confusion in propositional knowledge**: Ardelt's critique that Baltes & Staudinger confuse "having theoretical knowledge about wisdom" with "being a wise person" exposes an ontological error about the nature of knowledge itself. Propositional knowledge (descriptive knowledge) is treated as if it were sufficient for wisdom, when wisdom actually requires "interpretive knowledge"—the capacity to grasp significance. This distinction reveals the assumption that knowledge is fundamentally propositional and representational, which Vervaeke reframes through participatory knowing: the wise person doesn't just know propositions about wisdom, they embody wisdom through transformed self-world relations.

- **Wisdom as optimization not expertise**: Multiple theories mistakenly model wisdom on expertise (domain-specific skilled performance). Vervaeke argues this imports the wrong ontological framing: expertise is about efficiency within a bounded domain with stable rules, while wisdom is about dealing with ill-defined problems, combinatorial explosion, and fundamental uncertainty. Replacing expertise with rationality (specifically, relevance realization as optimization) shifts the ontology from "accumulation of domain knowledge" to "dynamic attunement to what matters across contexts"—a relational, responsive capacity rather than a possession.

- **The self as ecology vs. unified agent**: The implicit ontology across these theories (made most explicit in Ardelt) is that wisdom involves overcoming egocentrism through multiperspectival awareness and agape. This presupposes that the "normal" self is centered, unified, and individualistic—and wisdom involves transcending that frame. This aligns with Vervaeke's broader ontological claim that the Cartesian self-as-unified-rational-agent is itself an ontological error; the self is actually an ecology of processes that can be reorganized through participatory transformation.

- **The unexamined ontology of "common good"**: Vervaeke critiques Sternberg for placing "common good" as the ultimate aim of wisdom, noting this is anachronistic and culturally specific. This reveals an unexamined ontological commitment: that wisdom's telos can be specified propositionally and universally. Vervaeke suggests replacing it with the more general notion of "virtue + meaning in life," recognizing that wisdom's aim is participatory flourishing (transjective, context-dependent) rather than alignment with a predefined objective good.

## 1.3 Disciplinary Threads

### Cognitive Science

- **Relevance realization as the explanatory core**: All four theories implicitly invoke relevance realization machinery—Baltes & Staudinger explicitly cite "bounded rationality" (Herbert Simon), combinatorial explosion, dealing with ill-definedness, and the need for "flexible and efficient" reduction of complex information to essentials. This is relevance realization: the ongoing optimization of what to attend to, what to foreground, what to consider salient.

- **The Solomon effect and perspectival knowing**: Igor Grossman's research showing that third-person perspective-taking enhances wisdom (discussing with others or imagining discussing with internalized others) provides empirical support for the role of perspectival knowing. Taking multiple perspectives overcomes the "transparency and bias induction" of one's own perspective, aligning with the Stoic practice of internalizing the sage.

- **Understanding as grasping significance**: Ardelt's invocation of "interpretive knowledge" (from Kekes) as the ability to grasp the significance of descriptive knowledge points toward an account of understanding as central to wisdom. Vervaeke connects this to construal (how we frame situations) and relevance realization (determining what is significant). This suggests understanding is not another form of propositional knowledge but a dynamic process of meaning-making.

### Philosophy

- **Sophia and phronesis**: Multiple theories invoke Aristotle's distinction between theoretical wisdom (sophia) and practical wisdom (phronesis). Schwartz & Sharpe and Sternberg prioritize phronesis, while Vervaeke argues both are necessary and integrally connected—sophia provides the contemplative, reflective dimension that informs phronesis, and phronesis grounds sophia in lived reality.

- **Kekes' distinction: descriptive vs. interpretive knowledge**: This philosophical distinction (reported via Ardelt) reframes the knowledge question: wisdom is not just knowing facts but grasping their significance for one's life and development. This is a move away from purely propositional epistemology toward something closer to what the phenomenological tradition calls "lived meaning."

- **Foolishness requires independent theorization**: Vervaeke argues that defining foolishness merely as "absence of wisdom" or "imbalance" (Sternberg) is insufficient. A robust process theory needs an independent account of foolishness—how self-deception operates, how parasitic processing takes hold, how illusion becomes entrenched—in order to explain what wisdom must overcome. This is a methodological and ontological point: understanding a capacity requires understanding its opposite as a positive phenomenon, not a mere negation.

### Psychology of Meaning

- **Wisdom and meaning in life are distinct but connected**: Vervaeke notes (via Susan Wolf's framework) that wisdom cannot be reduced to virtue/morality alone; it must also connect to meaning in life. Ardelt's inclusion of agape (overcoming egocentrism, caring for others' flourishing) creates a bridge to meaning in life through transjective connectedness—you are coupled to what you find inherently valuable in a way that transcends purely subjective preference or objective value.

- **Transformative vs. theoretical knowledge**: Ardelt's critique that having knowledge about wisdom differs from being wise points to the necessity of transformative experience (LA Paul's framework, referenced but not elaborated by Vervaeke). The wise person has undergone self-transcendence, not just acquired information. This is the modal/existential dimension: wisdom is about who you are, not what you know.

- **Multi-dimensional personhood**: Ardelt's model (cognitive, reflective, affective dimensions mutually constraining and causally interacting) treats wisdom as an integrated transformation of the whole person, not a single trait or skill. This aligns with Vervaeke's participatory knowing framework—wisdom involves a reconfiguration of how you know yourself, others, and world across all modes of knowing.

### Wisdom & Rationality

- **Wisdom as rationality, not expertise**: Vervaeke argues that understanding wisdom through the lens of rationality (specifically, rationality as relevance realization plus recursive self-awareness) is more productive than modeling it as domain expertise. Expertise is S1 (automatic, domain-specific), while wisdom requires integrating S1 (phronesis, tacit understanding) with S2 (sophia, reflective awareness).

- **Baltes & Staudinger's five criteria map onto four kinds of knowing**: Propositional knowledge (knowing that), procedural knowledge (knowing how), contextualism as perspectival knowing (knowing from multiple viewpoints), fallibilism/humility replacing relativism, and applicability to uncertainty (requiring relevance realization). This shows wisdom requires all forms of knowing working together.

- **Sternberg's balance as optimization**: Sternberg's notion of "balance" (balancing interests—intrapersonal, interpersonal, extrapersonal—and balancing responses—adapting, shaping, selecting) is not spatial equilibrium but dynamic optimization. Vervaeke reads this as fundamentally about relevance realization: optimizing the fit between agent and environment across multiple constraints.

### Contemplative Practice

- **Internalizing the sage (Stoic practice)**: The empirical finding that imagining dialogue with a significant other enhances wisdom performance supports the Stoic practice of internalizing an idealized wise figure (e.g., Socrates). This allows one to provoke aporia (productive confusion) in oneself and challenge one's own transparency, facilitating transformative insight.

- **Platonic dialogue as wisdom practice**: The finding that discussing problems with others enhances wisdom (Baltes & Staudinger) connects to the Socratic/Platonic tradition of dialectic as a psychotechnology for overcoming self-deception and egocentrism. Dialogue is not just information exchange but a participatory process that restructures perspective.

- **Absence of a process theory reflects absence of practice framework**: Vervaeke's critique that contemporary theories lack process accounts implicitly points to the absence of specified contemplative practices or psychotechnologies. Ancient wisdom traditions centered practices (Stoic askesis, Buddhist meditation, Platonic dialectic) as the means of transformation; modern theories describe the endpoint without the path.

## 1.4 Key Concepts Introduced or Developed

- **Product Theory of Wisdom** — A theoretical approach that attempts to define what wisdom is by identifying wise people and articulating the characteristics, knowledge, or capacities they possess. *Ontological commitment: wisdom is a state or possession that can be described propositionally.*

- **Process Theory of Wisdom** — A theoretical approach that explains how one becomes wise by providing an independent account of foolishness, the mechanisms of self-deception, and the transformational processes that move one from foolishness to flourishing. Vervaeke argues this was central to ancient wisdom traditions (Plato, Aristotle, Buddhism, Stoicism) but is largely missing from contemporary psychology. *Ontological commitment: wisdom is a developmental trajectory and ongoing transformational practice, not a static endpoint.*

- **Descriptive vs. Interpretive Knowledge** (Kekes, via Ardelt) — Descriptive knowledge is propositional (knowing that X is the case), while interpretive knowledge is the capacity to grasp the significance of that knowledge for one's life, development, and flourishing. *Ontological commitment: wisdom requires a mode of knowing beyond the propositional—a hermeneutic capacity to construe meaning.*

- **Understanding (in wisdom context)** — The capacity to grasp significance, going beyond propositional knowledge. Vervaeke connects this to construal (how situations are framed) and relevance realization (what is foregrounded as mattering). Understanding is what allows knowledge to be existentially and practically meaningful. *Ontological commitment: meaning is not a property of propositions but emerges from the dynamic process of organism-environment coupling.*

- **Bounded Rationality** (Herbert Simon, invoked by Baltes & Staudinger) — Rationality that operates within the constraints of limited information, combinatorial explosion, ill-definedness, and the impossibility of exhaustive search. Wisdom operates within a frame that constrains and focuses attention, rather than attempting "unbounded rationality." *Ontological commitment: rationality is not algorithmic search over all possibilities but optimization within a dynamically constructed space of relevance.*

- **Metaheuristics for Life** (Baltes & Staudinger) — High-level strategies for orchestrating mind and behavior to deal with fundamental pragmatics of life (life planning, life management, life review). Vervaeke reads this as invoking relevance realization: metaheuristics guide what to pay attention to and how to prioritize. *Ontological commitment: wisdom involves recursive awareness of one's own cognitive strategies, not just first-order problem-solving.*

- **Cognitive, Reflective, Affective Dimensions** (Ardelt) — Three mutually interacting dimensions of the wise person: cognitive (comprehension of significance), reflective (multiperspectival awareness, self-examination, overcoming egocentrism), affective (compassion/agape, caring for others' flourishing). *Ontological commitment: the self is not a unified rational agent but an ecology of cognitive, perspectival, and affective processes that must be integrated for wisdom.*

- **Agape** (developed from Ardelt's "compassion") — Vervaeke interprets this as non-possessive, non-hierarchical caring aimed at the flourishing of the other for their own sake. Agape overcomes egocentrism attitudinally (not just perspectivally) and grounds meaning in life by transjectively coupling the agent to what is inherently valuable. *Ontological commitment: value is not purely subjective preference or objective fact but emerges in the participatory relation between agent and world.*

- **Balance as Optimization** (Sternberg) — Sternberg's "balance theory" uses "balance" to describe the optimization of fit between agent and environment: balancing interests (intrapersonal, interpersonal, extrapersonal) and responses (adapting, shaping, selecting environments). Vervaeke reads this as dynamic optimization, the core function of relevance realization. *Ontological commitment: rationality and wisdom are not rule-following but ongoing self-organizing optimization under multiple constraints.*

- **Tacit Understanding** (Sternberg, drawing on Polanyi) — Implicit, procedural knowledge that guides skillful action without explicit representation. Sternberg treats this as central to wisdom's ability to deal with practical, unformulated problems. Vervaeke connects it to subsidiary awareness and relevance realization. *Ontological commitment: most of cognition is not propositional but participatory and procedural; conscious articulation is the exception, not the norm.*

- **Common Good vs. Virtue + Meaning in Life** — Sternberg places "common good" as the ultimate goal of wisdom; Vervaeke critiques this as anachronistic (not all wise figures across cultures would affirm this liberal democratic value) and proposes instead "virtue + meaning in life" as more general and defensible. *Ontological commitment: wisdom's telos cannot be fixed propositionally in advance but must emerge from the participatory process of transformation within a given cultural and existential context.*

## 1.5 Argument Progression

This episode builds on Episode 43's examination of Schwartz & Sharpe's connection between wisdom and virtue, now expanding to survey the major psychological theories. Vervaeke is constructing a cumulative case: wisdom centrally involves relevance realization, multiple kinds of knowing (not just propositional), participatory transformation (not just theoretical knowledge), and connection to both virtue and meaning in life. The critique that all four theories are "product theories" lacking a "process theory" sets up the next move: Vervaeke will need to articulate his own integrative account that provides both—a definition of wisdom (product) grounded in an explanation of how one becomes wise (process). The emphasis on Ardelt's critique (theoretical knowledge vs. being wise) and the invocation of transformative experience foreshadows the need to connect wisdom to the machinery of self-transcendence, insight, and contemplative practice developed earlier in the series.

## 1.6 Open Wisdom Implications

- **The product/process distinction is itself ontological**: Modern psychology's focus on "what is wisdom" (product) vs. ancient philosophy's focus on "how to become wise" (process) reflects a deeper ontological difference. Product theories treat wisdom as a measurable trait or capacity—an object to be studied. Process theories treat wisdom as a developmental trajectory requiring participatory transformation. This matters for AI: are we trying to build systems that "have wisdom" (satisfy criteria) or systems that "become wise" (undergo transformative learning)? The process framing suggests wisdom might be less about static knowledge bases and more about ongoing self-reorganization in response to environment and feedback.

- **The modal confusion (knowing about vs. being) is directly relevant to AI design**: Ardelt's insight that having knowledge about wisdom differs from being wise maps onto a key AI question: can a system loaded with "wisdom literature" or trained on wise people's decisions actually be wise, or does wisdom require something like participatory knowing—a transformed way of being coupled to the world? Current AI has massive propositional/procedural knowledge but arguably lacks participatory knowing. This suggests that wisdom-relevant AI might require architectural innovations that enable something like "interpretive knowledge" (grasping significance) rather than just pattern matching.

- **Relevance realization as the common thread suggests an architectural principle**: That all four theories implicitly invoke relevance realization (bounded rationality, optimization, grasping significance, balancing) indicates this might be a core computational principle for wisdom. If wisdom is centrally about zeroing in on what matters across contexts, then AI systems aiming for wisdom-like behavior need robust, flexible relevance realization that can handle ill-defined problems, combinatorial explosion, and context sensitivity. This is already a challenge in AI alignment and agency research.

- **The necessity of a process theory suggests developmental AI paradigms**: If wisdom requires understanding how to move from foolishness to flourishing (not just defining the endpoint), then wisdom-relevant AI might need developmental learning paradigms—systems that can recognize when they are "foolish" (e.g., caught in parasitic processing, self-deceived), and undergo corrective transformations. This is closer to recursive self-improvement or meta-learning than supervised learning from static datasets. It implies AI that doesn't just execute but reflects on and reorganizes its own processes.

- **Wisdom's connection to meaning in life challenges purely instrumental AI**: The emphasis (especially in Ardelt and Sternberg) on wisdom being connected to meaning in life—not just task performance or moral behavior—challenges instrumental conceptions of AI. If wisdom is about "grasping significance," "overcoming egocentrism," and caring about flourishing (not just optimizing predefined objectives), then wisdom-like AI would need something like intrinsic valuing or transjective coupling, not just utility maximization. This is philosophically deep: can AI have meaning? Or can it at least support meaning-making in ways that respect the participatory, non-reducible nature of meaning?

- **The lack of a process theory in psychology reflects lack of operationalized practices**: That contemporary theories don't specify how one becomes wise suggests the transformation mechanisms are under-theorized. Ancient traditions had practices (meditation, dialectic, askesis), but modern psychology mostly describes outcomes. For AI, this is a design opportunity: if we can operationalize the transformative practices (e.g., perspective-taking, self-examination, construal shifting) as computational processes, we might build systems that undergo wisdom-like development. The contemplative traditions become a design inspiration, not just a philosophical reference.
