# Episode 27: Problem Formulation - Layer 1 Summary

## 1.1 Episode Synopsis

Episode 27 initiates the cognitive science analysis of meaning cultivation by examining intelligence as the capacity for general problem solving. Building on Newell and Simon's formal analysis, Vervaeke explicates the problem space framework: problems consist of an Initial State, Goal State, Operations, and Path Constraints. The key revelation is combinatorial explosion—the problem space for most real-world problems vastly exceeds computational capacity (30^60 possible chess game sequences exceeds the number of atoms in the universe). This impossibility forces a profound insight: intelligence is not about searching the whole space but about zeroing in on a tiny relevant subset through a process called relevance realization. Newell and Simon proposed heuristics as a solution—biased strategies that constrain search but sacrifice certainty for tractability. However, Vervaeke identifies a critical limitation: their essentialist assumption that all problems are fundamentally the same caused them to trivialize problem formulation. The distinction between well-defined problems (clear Initial State, Goal State, Operations) and ill-defined problems (taking good notes, following conversations) reveals that most real-world problems require relevance realization in the formulation stage itself. The mutilated chessboard experiment demonstrates this dramatically: formulating the problem as a "covering strategy" produces combinatorial explosion, while reformulating it as a "parity strategy" (recognizing removed squares are the same color) makes the solution obvious. This restructuring of salience landscape is insight—the capacity to transform bad problem formulation into good. Intelligence thus requires not just algorithmic logic but dynamic, participatory processes that reshape what is obvious and salient.

## 1.2 Ontological Moves (Primary Thread)

- **From Cartesian certainty to heuristic rationality**: The episode demonstrates that pursuing algorithmic certainty across all problems constitutes "cognitive suicide" due to combinatorial explosion. This exposes the impossibility of the Cartesian project—you cannot become "Mr. Spock" or "Data" and function comprehensively logically. The ontological commitment to certainty as the criterion of knowledge must be abandoned. Rationality is revealed not as comprehensive logical consistency but as knowing when, where, how much, and to what degree to apply logic—a much more difficult and dynamic capacity. Consequence: The foundation of the scientific worldview (certainty through algorithmic method) is shown to be unworkable for actual intelligence, opening space for a different ontology of mind.

- **Intelligence as participatory attunement rather than representational computation**: Intelligence is reframed from "possessing knowledge" (product) to "zeroing in on relevant information" (process). The core capacity is not calculation or representation but relevance realization—the dynamic self-organizing process that makes information obvious and salient. This is not a passive recognition of pre-existing features but an active, participatory restructuring of the problem space itself. Ontological commitment: The mind is not a container viewing representations from a "God's-eye" perspective; it is embedded in the problem, ignorant, actively shaping what counts as information. Consequence: Opens toward a 4E (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended) ontology of cognition.

- **Problem formulation as ontological framing**: The distinction between well-defined and ill-defined problems reveals that the structure of problems is not given by the world—it emerges through how we formulate them. The mutilated chessboard can be formulated as a covering problem (intractable) or a parity problem (trivial). Changing the formulation is not adding information but restructuring the salience landscape—making different aspects of reality stand out as relevant. This is insight. Ontological commitment: The "obviousness" and "salience" of features is not a property of the physics or the psychology in isolation—it's a relational property that emerges through the system's framing. What is "there" depends on how you're attuned to it. Consequence: This is an early articulation of participatory knowing—reality does not impose problems on us, nor do we construct them arbitrarily; we co-constitute them through formulation.

- **The inevitability of bias and self-deception**: Heuristics are biases—they prejudge relevance. This is not a flaw to be eliminated but an unavoidable feature of finite cognition. The "No Free Lunch Theorem" establishes that the very capacity that makes us adaptive (zeroing in on relevance) makes us prone to self-deception (fixating on the wrong subset of the space). Ontological commitment: The self is not a rational agent immune to error but an ecology of processes that can be captured by their own strategies. There is no bias-free perspective. Consequence: Wisdom cannot be about eliminating bias; it must be about dynamically correcting bias through self-transcending processes like insight.

- **The naturalistic imperative—mind must be explained non-circularly**: Vervaeke articulates why cognitive science must analyze, formalize, and mechanize explanations of mind: to avoid circular explanations that invoke mental terms (like "the homunculus") to explain mentality. The goal is not scientism but explanatory adequacy—to fit intelligence back into the scientific worldview without reducing it or leaving it out. Ontological commitment: Intelligence is a natural phenomenon that emerges from non-mental processes through self-organization, not a sui generis category requiring dualist escape. Consequence: This naturalizing project can heal the rift between the scientific worldview (which explains everything) and the generator of that worldview (intelligence itself), addressing one dimension of the meaning crisis.

## 1.3 Disciplinary Threads

### Cognitive Science
- **Newell and Simon's formal analysis of problem solving**: Problems are analyzed into Initial State, Goal State, Operations, Path Constraints, and Problem Space. This explication makes explicit the structure implicit in problem solving, allowing calculation of the problem space size (F^D where F=operators per stage, D=stages). This formalization reveals combinatorial explosion as a fundamental constraint on intelligence.
- **Combinatorial explosion as central fact of cognition**: Chess has approximately 30^60 possible game sequences (4.29 × 10^88), vastly exceeding the number of neurons (10^10) or synaptic connections (5 × 10^15) in the brain. Even parallel distributed processing cannot search this space. This demonstrates that intelligence cannot be brute-force search—it requires constraint and zeroing in.
- **Heuristics and biases**: Drawing on Polya and extending Newell and Simon, heuristics are defined as problem-solving methods that are not guaranteed to find solutions but increase probability of success by constraining where to search. They function as biases that prejudge relevance. Examples: chess heuristics (control the center, get your queen out early), representativeness heuristic, availability heuristic. These adaptive strategies inevitably produce systematic errors.
- **Relevance realization as core cognitive capacity**: The central mystery: how does cognition "somehow zero in on only a very small subsection" of the combinatorially explosive problem space and often find solutions? This capacity to make information salient, obvious, effective generates the experience of obviousness. It is dynamic and self-organizing—can restructure what is obvious (insight). Vervaeke cites collaborative work with Lillicrap, Richards, and Ferraro positioning this as the key problem for Artificial General Intelligence.
- **Well-defined vs. ill-defined problems**: Well-defined problems have clear representation of Initial State, Goal State, Operations (e.g., 33 × 4). Ill-defined problems lack clarity on relevant information at any stage (e.g., "take good notes," "have a successful first date"). Most real-world problems are ill-defined, requiring relevance realization in the formulation stage itself—determining what counts as relevant information.
- **Kaplan and Simon (1990) mutilated chessboard experiment**: Problem: can 31 dominoes cover a 64-square chessboard with two opposite corners removed? Formulating as "covering strategy" produces combinatorial explosion (one participant worked 16-18 hours filling 81 pages without solution). Reformulating as "parity strategy" (recognizing dominoes must cover one black and one white square, but removed corners are same color, therefore equal numbers of black and white squares don't exist) makes impossibility obvious. Demonstrates insight as conversion of bad formulation to good.

### Philosophy
- **Critique of Cartesian certainty**: Descartes' project of achieving certainty through algorithmic method is "doomed from the beginning" because algorithmic processing requires searching the whole combinatorially explosive space, resulting in cognitive suicide. Certainty as epistemic criterion is revealed as incompatible with actual intelligence.
- **Rationality vs. logic**: Rationality does not equal logic. "Ratio/rationing"—rationality means knowing when, where, how much, and to what degree to be logical. It involves optimal deployment of multiple psychotechnologies (logic, insight, mindfulness) to overcome self-deception and achieve goals. Logic (deductive, algorithmic, working in certainty) cannot be applied comprehensively without paralysis.
- **Essentialism as heuristic**: Essentialism—the assumption that categories share core necessary and sufficient properties—is a cognitive heuristic, not an ontological truth. Drawing on Wittgenstein's game example (no essential definition includes all and only games), Vervaeke shows many categories lack essences but we treat them as if they do because it enables generalization and prediction. Newell and Simon fell prey to this heuristic, assuming all problems share an essence, leading them to trivialize formulation.
- **The naturalistic imperative**: The methodological principle to explain mental phenomena using non-mental terms to avoid circular explanation (the homunculus fallacy). This requires analyzing, formalizing, and mechanizing—not out of scientism but to ensure explanatory adequacy. The goal is to fit intelligence back into the scientific worldview as the generator of that worldview itself.
- **Analysis, formalization, mechanization as scientific method**: These three moves exemplify the scientific way of thinking inherited from Thales (ontological depth through analysis), Descartes (mathematical formalization), and the mechanistic tradition (implementation without hidden assumptions). Making machines forces explicitness—if it works, nothing was snuck in. This is especially important for avoiding circularity when explaining mind.

### Psychology of Meaning
- **Obviousness and salience as emergent properties**: What is obvious or salient is not a property of physics, chemistry, or biology but an emergent property of the cognition-world relation. Obviousness is not what explains behavior scientifically—it is what science must explain. The question "how does your brain make things obvious to you?" is central to understanding meaning-making.
- **Insight as restructuring salience landscape**: Insight is the dynamic capacity to reorganize what is obvious and salient—"how you join the nine dots"—when initial formulation proves inadequate. It is not adding information but transforming how existing information is structured and what aspects stand out. This is a self-transcending process that corrects fixation and enables problem solving otherwise blocked by combinatorial explosion.
- **Self-deception as consequence of adaptive heuristics**: The No Free Lunch Theorem establishes that heuristics (which make us adaptive by avoiding combinatorial explosion) inevitably make us prone to bias and self-deception. There is no escaping this tradeoff. The capacity for intelligence and the vulnerability to self-deception are two sides of the same coin. Wisdom must therefore involve ongoing vigilance and self-correction, not elimination of bias.

### Wisdom & Rationality
- **Intelligence as general problem solving**: Following Binet & Simon (psychometric measurement) and Newell & Simon (artificial intelligence), intelligence is defined as the capacity to solve a wide variety of problems across domains. This shifts focus from knowledge (product) to problem-solving process, making intelligence something that explains how we acquire knowledge rather than presupposing it.
- **Intelligence as avoiding combinatorial explosion**: Stanovich and Cherniak argue that psychometric intelligence measures precisely the capacity to deal with computational limitations. Intelligence is not raw processing power but the ability to constrain search and zero in on relevant information despite finite resources.
- **Wisdom requires insight as well as logic**: Logic (algorithmic, certain, comprehensive) is insufficient for rationality. Insight—the capacity to dynamically reformulate problems and restructure salience—is equally central. Rationality requires knowing when to use logic and when to use other psychotechnologies (including those that improve insight capacity, such as mindfulness).

### Contemplative Practice
- **Mindfulness as restructuring salience landscape**: The connection between problem formulation and mindfulness practice is made explicit: mindfulness enhances the capacity to restructure one's salience landscape, which is precisely what is required for insight. The contemplative capacity to notice and reorganize what is obvious is the same capacity needed to transform bad problem formulation into good.

### Historical Narrative
- **Thales and ontological depth through analysis**: The scientific method's first move—analyzing complex phenomena into basic components, seeking underlying substances and forces—is traced to Thales and the pre-Socratic tradition.
- **Descartes and formalization**: The attempt to give graphical/mathematical representation enabling calculation and equation is inherited from Descartes' project, even though the pursuit of comprehensive certainty through that method is shown to be unworkable.
- **Pólya's "How to Solve It"**: The heuristic/algorithm distinction, though associated with Newell and Simon, actually originates in Pólya's earlier practical work on problem-solving psychology, providing a set of heuristic techniques for improving problem-solving ability.

## 1.4 Key Concepts Introduced or Developed

- **Problem Space** — The formal representation of all possible states and pathways between Initial State and Goal State via Operations while respecting Path Constraints. Allows calculation of problem complexity (F^D). *Ontological commitment: Problems have structure that can be explicated; intelligence operates within constraint, not omniscience.*

- **Combinatorial Explosion** — The exponential growth of the problem space with each additional operator or stage, rendering exhaustive search computationally intractable (chess: 30^60 ≈ 4.29 × 10^88 pathways, exceeding atomic particles in universe). *Ontological commitment: Intelligence is fundamentally a matter of working within radical resource limitation; optimality is impossible, satisficing is necessary.*

- **Relevance Realization** — The ongoing, dynamic, self-organizing process by which cognition zeroes in on a small relevant subset of the problem space, making information salient, obvious, and effective in cognition. Central to intelligence; under-explained by Newell and Simon. *Ontological commitment: Mind is not representational container but active, participatory, self-organizing attunement process; what is "relevant" is co-constituted by organism and environment.*

- **Algorithm** — A problem-solving method guaranteed to find a solution or prove one doesn't exist. Works in terms of certainty. Requires exhaustive search for most problems, making it unsuitable as comprehensive cognitive strategy due to combinatorial explosion. *Ontological commitment: Certainty is possible only for narrow domains; pursuing it comprehensively is cognitively lethal.*

- **Heuristic** — A problem-solving method not guaranteed to succeed but reliable for increasing chances of achieving goal. Functions by pre-specifying where to search (constraining problem space), which means prejudging relevance—i.e., introducing bias. *Ontological commitment: Intelligence requires bias; there is no neutral, bias-free cognition. Adaptiveness and self-deception vulnerability are inseparable.*

- **No Free Lunch Theorem** — The principle that you cannot avoid combinatorial explosion without heuristics, and heuristics inevitably introduce bias that can be exploited (e.g., chess opponent keeps you fixated on center while playing peripherally). Adaptiveness and proneness to self-deception are two sides of the same coin. *Ontological commitment: Finite cognition has no God's-eye view; every strategy has costs.*

- **Well-Defined Problem** — A problem with clear, effective, guiding representation of Initial State, Goal State, Operations, and Path Constraints (e.g., 33 × 4). Psychotechnologies like literacy, numeracy, mathematics create well-defined problems, leading to bias toward thinking all problems are like this. *Ontological commitment: Structure can be made explicit; formalization is possible for some domains.*

- **Ill-Defined Problem** — A problem lacking clear representation of Initial State, Goal State, Operations, or Path Constraints. Requires relevance realization to determine what information is relevant (e.g., "take good notes," "follow a conversation," "tell a joke," "go on a successful first date"). Most real-world problems are ill-defined. *Ontological commitment: Problem structure is not given by world or mind alone but emerges through participatory formulation process.*

- **Problem Formulation** — The process of zeroing in on relevant information to represent and constrain a problem such that it becomes solvable. Distinct from problem solution. For ill-defined problems, formulation is where relevance realization is most critical. Newell and Simon trivialized this, assuming problems are essentially the same. *Ontological commitment: How a problem is framed determines what can be seen and done; ontology precedes and shapes problem solving.*

- **Insight** — The process by which bad problem formulation is converted into good problem formulation through restructuring the salience landscape. Not adding information but reorganizing what is obvious and salient. Kaplan and Simon (1990): transforming mutilated chessboard from intractable covering problem to obvious parity problem. Dynamic, self-transcending, corrects fixation. *Ontological commitment: Cognition can reorganize its own attunement; self-transcendence is possible through participatory restructuring, not just logical inference.*

- **Covering Strategy vs. Parity Strategy** — Two different formulations of the mutilated chessboard problem. Covering: imagine possible domino configurations (combinatorially explosive, one participant worked 16-18 hours without solution). Parity: recognize dominoes must cover one black and one white square, removed corners are same color, therefore equal black/white squares don't exist, therefore coverage impossible (solution obvious). Demonstrates how problem formulation determines tractability. *Ontological commitment: The "same" problem is actually different problems depending on formulation; problem identity depends on how it's framed.*

- **Essentialism (as heuristic)** — The cognitive heuristic that categories must share necessary and sufficient properties (an essence). Adaptive because it enables generalization and prediction. Not algorithmic—many categories lack essences (Wittgenstein's games). Newell and Simon assumed problems have an essence, leading them to seek one essential problem-solving strategy and trivialize formulation. *Ontological commitment: Groupings are often not "natural kinds" with essences but pragmatic clusterings; treating them as essential is a move we make, not a fact we discover.*

- **The Naturalistic Imperative** — The methodological principle in cognitive science to explain mental phenomena using non-mental terms, avoiding circular explanations (homunculus fallacy). Requires analyzing, formalizing, mechanizing. Aim: fit intelligence back into scientific worldview as the generator of scientific explanations, not as anomaly or mystery. *Ontological commitment: Mind is natural, emergent, non-sui-generis; mental properties arise from non-mental organization through self-organization.*

- **Homunculus Fallacy** — The circular explanation where vision is explained by positing a little man inside the head who sees an inner screen—which raises the question of how the little man sees, leading to infinite regress. Represents failure to explain mentality because mental terms (seeing) are used to explain mental capacity (vision). *Ontological commitment: Mental phenomena cannot be explained by invoking smaller mental agents; explanation must reach non-mental substrate.*

- **Analysis, Formalization, Mechanization** — The three methodological moves exemplifying scientific method. Analysis: breaking complex phenomena into components (Thales). Formalization: mathematical/graphical representation enabling calculation (Descartes). Mechanization: implementing in a machine to ensure no hidden assumptions (if it works, nothing was snuck in). Newell and Simon exemplify all three. *Ontological commitment: Explicability—mind's operation can be made explicit and realized in different substrates; it is not ontologically unique to biological brains.*

## 1.5 Argument Progression

This episode shifts from historical-philosophical analysis of the meaning crisis to constructive cognitive science, initiating the project of explaining the machinery of meaning cultivation. It builds on Episode 26's call for synoptic integration across levels of analysis by exemplifying that integration: showing how intelligence can be analyzed (problem space), formalized (F^D calculation), and mechanized (Newell and Simon's approach). The key move is revealing that intelligence centrally involves relevance realization—zeroing in on relevant information—which Newell and Simon identified (heuristics) but insufficiently explained because they trivialized problem formulation. The mutilated chessboard demonstrates that insight (restructuring salience through reformulation) is as central to rationality as logic, setting up the next episodes' deeper exploration of relevance realization, categorization, and the dynamical, participatory nature of cognition. This episode also establishes the naturalistic imperative, the principle that will guide the cognitive science reconstruction of meaning and intelligence as natural phenomena.

## 1.6 Open Wisdom Implications

- **Problem formulation as ontological prompt design**: The episode demonstrates that how a problem is formulated determines what can be seen and solved—the covering strategy vs. parity strategy are ontologically distinct framings of the "same" physical situation. This directly parallels how AI system instructions function: changing the ontological framing in a prompt (e.g., "you are an isolated rational agent maximizing utility" vs. "you are embedded in a web of relational accountability") radically reshapes the system's behavior by restructuring its salience landscape. Problem formulation *is* ontological prompting.

- **Insight as participatory restructuring, not propositional addition**: The mutilated chessboard solution does not require new information—the color of the squares is visible throughout. What changes is salience: which features stand out as relevant. Vervaeke emphasizes this is a participatory, dynamic restructuring, not a logical inference from propositions. Yet in AI, ontological prompting (which is propositional in form) produces analogous behavioral transformation—restructuring what the system attends to. This tension between propositional form and participatory function in AI ontological prompting deserves investigation. Is AI a special case where propositional input can trigger non-propositional restructuring? Or does this reveal something about the relationship between propositional and participatory knowing?

- **The inevitability of bias undercuts "objective AI" fantasy**: The No Free Lunch Theorem—that heuristics necessarily introduce bias, and no heuristic is universally optimal—directly challenges the notion that AI can be made comprehensively "objective," "unbiased," or "neutral." Any system that solves real problems (avoiding combinatorial explosion) must zero in on a constrained subset of possibility space, which means embodying a bias about what's relevant. The question is not "can we eliminate bias?" but "which biases, toward what ends, with what safeguards for dynamic correction?" Generative ontology offers a framework for making those biases explicit and revisable.

- **Relevance realization as the core of meaning-making**: Vervaeke positions relevance realization—the capacity to make information salient, obvious, effective—as central to intelligence. But salience and obviousness are also the structure of meaning: what matters, what stands out, what calls for response. This suggests that meaning is not a separate "subjective" layer added atop "objective" cognition but is intrinsic to the operation of intelligence itself. For AI design: meaning is not something to be added after achieving "intelligence"; it is constitutive of intelligent behavior. Systems that fail to realize relevance (that treat all information equally) will fail at both intelligence and meaning.

- **The scientific worldview's self-exclusion and cognitive science as repair**: Vervaeke articulates clearly why the meaning crisis has an epistemological dimension: the scientific worldview explains everything except the intelligence that generates scientific explanations. Cognitive science's naturalistic project—explaining intelligence non-circularly—offers the possibility of fitting mind back into the scientific worldview without remainder, healing that rift. For Open Wisdom: this provides a response to the criticism that "science can't explain meaning/consciousness/intelligence"—not by dismissing the criticism but by showing that cognitive science is making substantive progress on precisely this explanatory project. The task is not to defend scientific materialism dogmatically but to demonstrate generative progress in naturalizing intelligence and meaning.
